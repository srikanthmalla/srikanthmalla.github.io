## The core mental model

The forward process adds Gaussian noise on a schedule, and the single property that
everything else is built on is that its **marginal has a closed form**:

$$
q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\; (1-\bar\alpha_t) I\right),
\qquad
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon .
$$

You can jump to any noise level in one step without simulating the chain. That is what makes
training a cheap Monte Carlo estimate: sample $x_0$, sample $t$, sample $\varepsilon$, take one
gradient step. Without the closed form you would have to unroll hundreds of steps per training
example and the whole approach would be uncompetitive. Every design choice in the forward process
exists to protect this property.

Gaussian is not an aesthetic preference either. Three things have to hold simultaneously, and the
Gaussian is essentially the only convenient family where they all do. **Marginals stay in the
family** under composition, giving the closed form above. **The reverse of a sufficiently small
forward step is itself approximately Gaussian** — a classical result going back to Feller — so the
reverse process is representable by a model that predicts a mean and a variance, rather than
something arbitrarily complicated. And **the score of a Gaussian-smoothed distribution is
recoverable from the conditional mean**, which is Tweedie's formula:

$$
\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t] = \frac{x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} .
$$

Read that identity in the direction that matters: **a denoiser is a score model**. Train a network
to predict the clean sample (or equivalently the noise) under MSE, and the optimum is the
conditional expectation, which is an affine function of $\nabla \log p(x_t)$. You did not train a
denoiser and then cleverly repurpose it; the denoiser was always estimating the score of the
noise-perturbed data distribution, and the score is exactly the quantity the reverse SDE needs.
This is the hinge the entire field turns on, and it is why "denoising diffusion" and "score-based
generative modelling" turned out to be the same paper written twice.

<Callout type="bridge" title="the two lineages are one">
  DDPM derives a variational bound on a Markov chain. Score matching estimates
  $\nabla \log p$ at multiple noise levels. Tweedie's formula is the change of variables that maps
  one to the other, which is why VP and VE formulations differ only by scaling and a
  reparameterisation of time.
</Callout>

## Numbers worth memorizing

| Quantity                       | Definition / value                                        |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Marginal                       | $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\varepsilon$ |
| $\bar\alpha_t$                 | $\prod_{s\le t}(1-\beta_s)$                                |
| **SNR**                        | $\mathrm{SNR}(t) = \bar\alpha_t / (1-\bar\alpha_t)$        |
| Tweedie                        | $\hat x_0 = (x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)s_\theta)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ |
| Score from noise prediction    | $s_\theta = -\varepsilon_\theta/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$     |
| DDPM default                   | $T = 1000$, $\beta$ linear $10^{-4} \to 0.02$              |
| $\bar\alpha_T$ (DDPM linear)   | $\approx 4\times10^{-5}$ — **not zero**                    |
| Karras $\sigma$ range          | $0.002 \to 80$                                             |
| log-SNR $= 0$                  | half signal, half noise — the middle of the process        |

Two formulations, one process:

| | Variance preserving (DDPM) | Variance exploding (NCSN) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marginal | $\sqrt{\bar\alpha}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\varepsilon$ | $x_0 + \sigma\varepsilon$ |
| $\mathrm{Var}[x_t]$ | held at ~1 | grows to $\sigma_{\max}^2$ |
| Terminal | $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ | $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\max}^2 I)$ |
| Relation | $x_t^{VP} = x_t^{VE}/\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}$ with $\sigma^2 = (1-\bar\alpha)/\bar\alpha$ | |

<Callout type="trap" title="the terminal SNR is not zero">
  With the standard linear schedule $\bar\alpha_T \approx 4\times 10^{-5}$, so $x_T$ still carries a
  faint trace of $x_0$ — in particular its **mean brightness**. Training sees that leak; sampling
  starts from pure $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and does not. The result is the well-documented inability of
  such models to produce very dark or very bright images, and it is fixed by rescaling the schedule
  to enforce zero terminal SNR.
</Callout>

## Critical thinking

<Probe q="Why Gaussian noise? Give the reasons that would actually break if you chose something else.">

Three requirements have to hold at once.

1. **Closed-form marginals.** Composing Gaussians gives a Gaussian, so $q(x_t \mid x_0)$ is known
   analytically and training samples $t$ directly. With almost any other noise family you would
   have to simulate the chain per example — hundreds of forward passes per gradient step.
2. **Gaussian reverse steps.** For a small enough step, the reverse conditional $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$
   is approximately Gaussian. This is what licenses a model that outputs a mean (and optionally a
   variance) rather than an arbitrary distribution. If reverse steps were not approximately
   Gaussian you would need a far more expressive per-step model, and the step count could not be
   traded against quality the way it is.
3. **Tweedie.** The score is recoverable from the posterior mean, so an MSE-trained denoiser *is* a
   score model. This is a property of the exponential family, not a general fact.

Discrete corruption processes — masking, absorbing states, multinomial diffusion — drop the second
and third and replace them with a categorical machinery. They work, and they are the right choice
for genuinely discrete data, but they lose the ODE view in Module 4 and with it most of the fast
samplers.

</Probe>

<Probe q="What exactly does Tweedie's formula buy you?">

It collapses two apparently different research programmes into one, and it tells you what your
network is really computing.

Concretely: train $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ under $\mathbb{E}\lVert\varepsilon -
\varepsilon_\theta\rVert^2$. The Bayes-optimal predictor of an MSE objective is the conditional
expectation, so at convergence $\varepsilon_\theta = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t]$. Substituting
the forward marginal and rearranging,

$$
\nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t) \;=\; -\frac{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} .
$$

So the noise prediction is the score up to a known scalar. Three consequences follow immediately.
The reverse SDE and the probability-flow ODE (Module 4) can be written directly in terms of the
network output, so every ODE solver in the numerical analysis literature becomes available.
Guidance (Module 6) is score arithmetic — you add scaled score terms, which is only meaningful
because the output *is* a score. And you can convert freely between $\varepsilon$-, $x_0$-, $v$-
and score-parameterisations at inference, because they are affine reparameterisations of one
quantity (Module 2).

The mental shift worth making: the network is not learning "how to remove noise". It is learning a
vector field — the gradient of the log-density of the data blurred at scale $t$ — and denoising is
simply the cheapest supervised way to fit it.

</Probe>

<Probe q="Are VP and VE actually different models?">

No. They are the same family under a change of variables, and treating them as rival designs is a
historical artefact of the two lineages meeting late.

Set $\sigma_t^2 = (1-\bar\alpha_t)/\bar\alpha_t$. Then the VP sample is exactly the VE sample
rescaled: $x^{VP}_t = x^{VE}_t / \sqrt{1+\sigma_t^2}$, and the scores relate by the same factor.
Karras et al. made this explicit by writing all of them as one ODE with a choice of scaling
$s(t)$ and noise $\sigma(t)$, then arguing those choices should be made on numerical grounds
rather than probabilistic ones.

What genuinely differs is **conditioning of the numerics**, and that is not nothing. VP keeps the
network's input variance around 1 at every noise level, which is friendly to normalisation layers.
VE inputs span orders of magnitude, so it needs explicit input scaling and a $\sigma$-dependent
output skip connection to stay well-conditioned — the preconditioning that the EDM formulation
makes explicit.

So the right question is never "VP or VE" but "what scaling keeps the network's inputs, targets and
loss weighting well-conditioned across noise levels", which is Module 2.

</Probe>

<Probe q="Why can many diffusion models not generate a genuinely black image?">

Because the terminal signal-to-noise ratio is not actually zero, so training and sampling start
from different distributions.

With the standard linear schedule, $\bar\alpha_T \approx 4\times10^{-5}$, giving
$\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T} \approx 0.0063$. Tiny — but the mean brightness of an image is a very
low-frequency, high-variance-across-dataset quantity, so that small surviving component is
informative. During training the model always sees an $x_T$ that quietly encodes the image's mean
level, and it learns to rely on it. At sampling time you hand it pure $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, whose
mean is zero. The model duly produces images with roughly average brightness, and cannot be
prompted out of it.

The fix is to rescale the schedule so $\bar\alpha_T = 0$ exactly, which requires also switching to
a $v$-parameterisation (Module 2), because $\varepsilon$-prediction is degenerate at zero SNR —
if there is no signal, predicting the noise is trivial and gives no gradient toward $x_0$.

The general lesson is worth more than the specific bug: **any mismatch between the training
distribution of $x_T$ and the sampling prior shows up as a systematic bias in the output**, and
because it lives at the highest noise level it affects the most global attributes — brightness,
colour balance, overall composition.

</Probe>

## Self-check

<SelfCheck q="Write the forward marginal and say which property of it makes training cheap.">

$x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$ with
$\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s \le t}(1-\beta_s)$. Being closed-form means you can jump to any $t$ in one
step, so training is: sample $x_0$, sample $t$, sample $\varepsilon$, one forward pass. Without it
you would simulate the chain per example and pay hundreds of passes per gradient step.

</SelfCheck>

<SelfCheck q="State Tweedie's formula and convert a noise prediction into a score.">

$\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t] = \bigl(x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\nabla\log p(x_t)\bigr)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$.
Rearranged for a noise-prediction network,
$\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) = -\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$. So an MSE-trained
denoiser is a score model up to a known scalar — the identity that makes ODE samplers and guidance
possible.

</SelfCheck>

<SelfCheck q="Give the three properties of Gaussian noise that the framework depends on.">

Closed-form composition, so marginals stay Gaussian and training can sample $t$ directly; small
reverse steps are approximately Gaussian, so a mean-predicting network suffices; and Tweedie, so
the score is recoverable from the posterior mean. Discrete corruption processes give up the second
and third, which is why they lose the ODE view and the fast solvers built on it.

</SelfCheck>

<SelfCheck q="Relate VP and VE.">

They are one process under a change of variables: with $\sigma_t^2 = (1-\bar\alpha_t)/\bar\alpha_t$,
$x^{VP}_t = x^{VE}_t/\sqrt{1+\sigma_t^2}$. What genuinely differs is numerical conditioning — VP
holds input variance near 1, while VE spans orders of magnitude and needs explicit input scaling
and a $\sigma$-dependent skip connection to stay well-behaved.

</SelfCheck>

<SelfCheck q="Why does a non-zero terminal SNR bias the output, and why does fixing it force a parameterization change?">

$\bar\alpha_T \approx 4\times10^{-5}$ leaves a faint trace of $x_0$ in $x_T$ — enough to encode mean
brightness. Training uses that leak; sampling starts from pure noise which has none, so outputs
regress to average brightness. Enforcing $\bar\alpha_T = 0$ fixes the mismatch, but at zero SNR
predicting $\varepsilon$ is trivial and carries no information about $x_0$, so the objective must
move to $v$-prediction.

</SelfCheck>
