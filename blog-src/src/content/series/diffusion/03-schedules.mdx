## The core mental model

Timestep index is a bad coordinate. It is an arbitrary discretisation of a continuous process, and
two schedules with the same $T$ can put wildly different amounts of work in the same range of $t$.
The coordinate that means something is **log-SNR**, $\lambda_t = \log\bigl(\bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t)\bigr)$,
because it measures how much of the signal actually survives. Once you plot everything against
$\lambda$, schedule design becomes legible: a schedule is just a choice of how fast to sweep
$\lambda$ from about $+10$ (nearly clean) to about $-10$ (nearly pure noise), and how much time to
spend in each band.

<FigRef for="lamsweep">The linear $\beta$ schedule from DDPM sweeps $\lambda$ very unevenly — it
spends a large fraction of its steps in a regime where the image is already destroyed</FigRef> and
there is nothing left to learn. The
cosine schedule was introduced precisely to fix that, holding more of the budget in the middle
where structure is decided. Everything since is a variation on the same question: given a fixed
number of training steps and a fixed number of sampling steps, where in log-SNR should they go?

The part that trips people up when they scale is that **the answer depends on resolution**. Adding
noise of a fixed variance per pixel destroys far less information in a large image than a small
one, because natural images are spatially redundant: you can average neighbouring pixels to recover
the low frequencies, and the more pixels you have, the more averaging is available. So at
1024×1024, a noise level that would have obliterated a 64×64 image still leaves the global
composition perfectly legible. Train a high-resolution model on a schedule tuned at low resolution
and you will spend nearly all of your capacity on noise levels where the picture is already
essentially decided — the model never learns to make global choices, because it never sees a
training example where the global content is genuinely uncertain. The fix is to **shift the
schedule toward higher noise as resolution grows**, by roughly $\log(n/n_{\text{ref}})$ in log-SNR
for linear dimension $n$.

<Callout type="bridge" title="the frequency view">
  Diffusion is a coarse-to-fine process in frequency: high noise destroys high frequencies first
  because they have the least energy in natural images. A schedule is therefore a plan for the
  order in which frequency bands get decided, and resolution changes which noise level corresponds
  to which band.
</Callout>

## The formulas and the numbers

Schedule quantities, all expressed against log-SNR rather than against the timestep index:

| Quantity                     | Definition / value                                              |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| log-SNR                      | $\lambda_t = \log\!\bigl(\bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t)\bigr)$    |
| Useful range                 | $\lambda \in [-10, +10]$ covers essentially everything           |
| $\lambda = 0$                | half signal, half noise — where structure is decided             |
| Cosine schedule              | $\bar\alpha_t = \cos^2\!\bigl(\tfrac{t/T + s}{1+s}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)$, $s = 0.008$ |
| Resolution shift             | $\lambda \mathrel{-}= 2\log(n/n_{\text{ref}})$ for linear size $n$ |
| 64 → 1024 shift              | $2\log 16 \approx 5.5$ nats of log-SNR                            |
| Karras $\sigma$ distribution | $\log\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(P_{\text{mean}}, P_{\text{std}})$, $(-1.2, 1.2)$ |

Schedule comparison, informally:

| Schedule            | Behaviour                                       | Where it fails            |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Linear $\beta$      | sweeps $\lambda$ fast at the end                | wastes steps on destroyed images; non-zero terminal SNR |
| Cosine              | more budget in the middle                       | still resolution-blind    |
| Shifted cosine      | cosine plus a resolution-dependent offset       | needs the shift tuned     |
| Karras / EDM        | designed in $\sigma$ with explicit $\log\sigma$ sampling | requires the preconditioning to match |
| Zero terminal SNR   | rescaled so $\bar\alpha_T = 0$                  | forces $v$-prediction     |

<Callout type="trap" title="reusing a 256px schedule at 1024px">
  The most common scaling failure. At the higher resolution most of the schedule sits in a regime
  where the composition is already determined, so the model gets almost no training signal about
  global structure and produces high-frequency-plausible, globally-incoherent images. The symptom
  is "the textures look great and the layout is nonsense".
</Callout>

## Critical thinking

<Probe q="Why does the same noise level destroy less information at higher resolution?">

Because natural images are spatially redundant and the noise is not.

Add i.i.d. noise of variance $\sigma^2$ per pixel. Now average over a $k \times k$ block. The signal
survives almost intact — neighbouring pixels are highly correlated, so their mean is close to the
true local mean — while the noise variance falls to $\sigma^2/k^2$. The effective SNR of the
low-frequency content therefore improves by $k^2$ simply because more pixels are available to
average.

Concretely, doubling the linear resolution gives four times as many pixels per unit of image
content, so the recoverable low-frequency signal gains a factor of about 4 in SNR — roughly 1.4
nats of log-SNR per doubling, or about 5.5 nats going from 64 to 1024.

Consequences worth stating:

- A fixed schedule becomes *effectively less noisy* as resolution grows, so the model spends its
  capacity in the wrong place.
- The fix is a shift, not a rescale: subtract about $2\log(n/n_{\text{ref}})$ from log-SNR so that
  the same *information* is destroyed at the same point in the schedule.
- This also explains why latent diffusion (Module 8) needs less shifting — the latent has already
  removed much of the spatial redundancy, so its effective resolution is much smaller than the
  pixel resolution suggests.

</Probe>

<Probe q="Uniform sampling of t is a bad default. Why, and what do you do instead?">

Because uniform in $t$ is not uniform in anything meaningful, and $t$ is not the axis on which the
learning problem is defined.

The mapping $t \mapsto \lambda$ is highly non-linear, so uniform $t$ concentrates samples wherever
the schedule happens to move slowly through log-SNR. With the linear schedule that is the
high-noise end, where the target is nearly degenerate and there is little to learn — you spend a
large share of your compute on the easiest region.

Three better options:

1. **Uniform in log-SNR.** Sample $\lambda$ uniformly on its useful range and invert the schedule
   to get $t$. Simple, principled, and usually an improvement on its own.
2. **A distribution over $\log\sigma$.** EDM samples $\log\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(-1.2, 1.2)$,
   deliberately concentrating on the middle band where the model is most uncertain and the
   sampler will later spend most of its steps.
3. **Importance sampling by observed loss.** Maintain a running estimate of loss per $\lambda$ bin
   and sample proportionally, which is a variance-reduction technique for the gradient estimator
   and adapts automatically as training progresses.

Note the interaction with Module 2: changing the $t$ distribution and changing the loss weighting
have overlapping effects, since both scale the contribution of a noise level to the gradient. Do
one deliberately rather than both accidentally, or you will not be able to attribute the result.

</Probe>

<Probe q="Given a fixed step budget at sampling time, where should the steps go?">

Where the trajectory has the most curvature, which is not where the noise is highest.

The probability-flow ODE (Module 4) is close to linear at very high noise — the score points more
or less straight at the data mean, and large steps are accurate. It is also close to linear at very
low noise, where the remaining update is a small refinement. The curvature is concentrated in the
middle band, near $\lambda \approx 0$, which is exactly where the sample commits to what it is
going to be.

This is why practical step schedules are not uniform in $t$:

- **Karras spacing** distributes $\sigma$ according to $\sigma_i \propto (\sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho} +
  \frac{i}{N-1}(\sigma_{\min}^{1/\rho} - \sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho}))^{\rho}$ with $\rho = 7$, which
  concentrates steps at low $\sigma$ while still taking a few large ones early.
- Uniform-in-$\lambda$ spacing is a reasonable default and much better than uniform in $t$.
- Very few steps at the very start is safe; very few in the middle is what produces the
  characteristic "the composition changed between step counts" instability.

The general framing is Module 4's: this is a numerical integration problem, and step placement is
adaptive quadrature. You put nodes where the integrand varies.

</Probe>

<Probe q="Your 1024px model produces gorgeous texture and incoherent layout. What do you check first?">

The schedule, before anything about architecture or data.

The signature is diagnostic. Global incoherence with good local detail means the model was never
trained in the regime where global structure is uncertain — it has effectively only ever seen
"refine this mostly-decided image" problems. That is exactly what happens when a schedule tuned at
low resolution is reused at high resolution, because the same per-pixel noise destroys far less
information when there are more pixels to average over.

**Check:** plot the *effective* log-SNR of your training distribution at this resolution. Better,
look at samples of $x_t$ at several $t$ and ask a human at what point the composition becomes
unrecoverable. If that point is at the extreme end of your schedule, almost all your training
budget is downstream of the decision you care about.

**Fix:** shift the schedule toward higher noise by roughly $2\log(n/n_{\text{ref}})$ in log-SNR.
Related mitigations that address the same problem from other directions: cascaded generation (make
global decisions at low resolution and upsample), and training at multiple resolutions with the
shift applied per resolution.

The general principle worth keeping: **when a model fails at one spatial scale and not another,
suspect the noise schedule before the architecture**, because the schedule is what determines which
scale each training example is asking about.

</Probe>

## Self-check

<SelfCheck q="Why is log-SNR the right time axis?">

completely different amounts of work in the same range of $t$. log-SNR
$\lambda = \log(\bar\alpha/(1-\bar\alpha))$ measures how much signal actually survives, so a
schedule becomes legible as a plan for sweeping $\lambda$ from about $+10$ to $-10$ and choosing how
long to linger in each band.

</SelfCheck>

<SelfCheck q="Quantify how resolution changes the effective noise level.">

Averaging a $k\times k$ block leaves correlated signal nearly intact while cutting noise variance to
$\sigma^2/k^2$, so low-frequency SNR improves by about $k^2$. Doubling linear resolution is roughly
1.4 nats of log-SNR; 64 → 1024 is about $2\log 16 \approx 5.5$ nats. Schedules must shift toward
higher noise by that much to destroy the same *information* at the same point.

</SelfCheck>

<SelfCheck q="What is wrong with sampling t uniformly?">

The map $t \mapsto \lambda$ is strongly non-linear, so uniform $t$ over-samples wherever the
schedule moves slowly through log-SNR — with a linear schedule, the high-noise end where targets are
nearly degenerate. Better: sample uniformly in log-SNR, use EDM's $\log\sigma \sim
\mathcal{N}(-1.2,1.2)$, or importance-sample by observed per-bin loss. Beware doing this *and*
changing the loss weighting at once, since their effects overlap.

</SelfCheck>

<SelfCheck q="Given 20 sampling steps, where do they go and why?">

curvature and the sample commits to its content. The trajectory is nearly linear at very high noise
(the score points at the data mean) and at very low noise (small refinements), so large steps are
accurate there. Karras spacing with $\rho = 7$ is the standard concrete choice; uniform-in-$\lambda$
is a decent default and much better than uniform in $t$.

</SelfCheck>
